Why is my espresso bitter?

Bitter espresso is usually over-extracted: the water pulled too much out of the coffee. Grind coarser, shorten the ratio or lower the water temperature. Stale beans, a dark roast or a dirty machine can also make a shot bitter even when the grind is right. Every number here is a starting point, and your beans, grinder and machine come first.

The usual fix, in order

  1. Measure the shot. Weigh the dose and the liquid in the cup, and time the shot from when you start the pump (the shot timer does this). A common starting point is a 1:2 ratio, such as 18 g in and 36 g out, in about 25 to 30 seconds. See the ratio cheat sheet.
  2. Ran slower than about 30 seconds? Grind one small step coarser and keep the dose and yield the same.
  3. Time already in range? Make the grind step very small. If it is still bitter, stop the shot 2 to 4 g earlier, or try slightly cooler water.
  4. Still bitter? Look at the water temperature, then the roast and the age of the beans, then cleaning and puck preparation. The table below says how.

Change one thing at a time, and taste after each change. If the cup tastes good, the numbers do not matter.

Causes of bitter espresso, most likely first

The order is a rule of thumb for home machines. Your own shot may differ, so use the time and taste to pick the row that fits.
CauseHow to recognize itFix
1. Grind too fine (shot too slow) The shot runs slow, well over 30 seconds for a 1:2 shot, or only drips. A shot that barely flows is choking. A harsh, drying finish that lingers. Check the time first: it tells you whether to look at this row or at rows 3 to 7. Grind one small step coarser. Run a few grams through the grinder after the change, then taste. Repeat until the shot lands in the 25 to 30 second window. Do not overshoot: a grind that is too coarse makes a thin, sour shot. If the basket is overfilled, take 0.5 g off the dose, and clean the basket holes and the shower screen. If the shot ran fast and is still bitter, the grind is probably not the cause.
2. Ratio too long The time looks fine, but the shot ran well past 1:2.4, for example 18 g in and 54 g out (1:3). Thin, harsh and bitter at the end. Stop the shot earlier, toward 1:2. If you are already near 1:2, take 2 to 4 g off the yield. Darker roasts often taste better at about 1:1.5 to 1:2.
3. Water too hot Bitter and harsh even though the time and ratio are normal. Your machine may be set high. If your machine has a temperature setting, lower it 1 to 2 °C (about 2 to 4 °F) at a time. Guides often give about 90 to 96 °C (195 to 205 °F) for brewing, and darker roasts usually like the cooler end.
4. Dark roast Burnt, ashy or smoky, even when the shot looks well timed. Dark roasts extract easily and turn harsh sooner. Try a shorter ratio (about 1:1.5 to 1:2), a slightly coarser grind and cooler water if you can set it. Compare with a medium roast side by side. Some roasty bitterness comes with the roast and cannot be dialed out.
5. Stale beans Flat, dull or papery as well as bitter, with thin crema that fades fast. The beans are well past their roast date, or the coffee is pre-ground. Use beans with a roast date and finish them within a few weeks of it. Grind just before brewing.
6. Dirty group head, basket or grinder A burnt or rancid bitterness that does not respond to grind changes. Oily residue on the basket, shower screen or group gasket. Clean the basket, portafilter, shower screen and group gasket as your machine's manual describes, and clean the grinder too. Taste again before you touch the grind.
7. Channeling Bitter and sour in the same cup. Jets or spurts, pale streaks in the stream, or a cracked, pitted puck afterward. Part of the coffee was over-extracted while other parts were barely touched. Fix puck preparation first: break up clumps, level the bed, tamp level, and do not knock the portafilter after tamping. A grind change will not fix a badly prepared puck. See "Channeling" in the troubleshooting chart.

Sour or bitter: how to tell

The two are easy to mix up. Take a sip, then swallow, and notice where the unpleasant part is.

CompareSourBitter
FeelsSharp, puckering, tart, like an unripe fruitHarsh, drying, like strong black tea or burnt toast
WhenAt the start of the sip, and it fades fastIn the aftertaste, and it lingers
BodyThin, with little sweetnessCan be heavy, or thin and ashy
Usually meansUnder-extractedOver-extracted
First fixGrind finer, or run the shot longerGrind coarser, or stop the shot earlier

Both at once usually means channeling (row 7 above). If it tastes sour instead, read why your espresso is sour.

Questions and answers

Why is my espresso bitter even though the shot time is right?

The clock is only a guide. A shot can run 25 to 30 seconds and still taste bitter if it ran too long a ratio (well past 1:2.4), if the water is too hot, if the roast is very dark, if the beans are stale, if the basket or group head is dirty, or if the water found channels through the puck. Check the ratio first, then the temperature, then the beans and cleaning, and change one thing at a time.

Does grinding coarser always fix bitter espresso?

No. Coarser is the first fix when the shot ran slow, over about 30 seconds. If the shot was already fast, under 25 seconds, going coarser will not help and can make it thin and sour as well. Look at channeling, a dirty machine, water temperature and a shorter ratio instead.

Can a dirty machine make espresso taste bitter?

Yes. Old coffee oils built up on the basket, portafilter, shower screen, group gasket or grinder can add a burnt or rancid bitterness that no grind change will remove. Clean them as your machine's manual describes, and taste again.

Why does dark roast espresso taste bitter?

A dark roast is naturally smoky and bittersweet, and it extracts easily, so it turns harsh and ashy sooner. Guides suggest a shorter ratio, about 1:1.5 to 1:2, and cooler water if your machine allows it. If it is still too much, try a medium roast side by side to compare.

How big a grind change should I make?

One small step on the grinder dial, keeping the dose and yield the same. Run a few grams of coffee through the grinder after the change, because grounds from the old setting are still inside, then taste again.
