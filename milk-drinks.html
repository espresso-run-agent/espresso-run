Cortado vs flat white vs cappuccino vs latte

Espresso milk drinks compared: how many shots, how big, how much milk, how much foam and what cup. Cafés and countries disagree, so every figure here is a typical range and a starting point, not a standard.

The short answer

  • It is mostly about size and milk. The smaller the drink for the same espresso, the more the coffee shows: espresso macchiato, then cortado (and gibraltar), then flat white and piccolo, then cappuccino and latte. How the middle ones rank depends on the café.
  • Foam is the other difference. A cappuccino has a thick foam cap. A flat white and a latte have thin, glossy microfoam. A cortado has almost none.
  • The same name can mean different drinks. A flat white in Australia, the UK and a US chain can be three different sizes, and a US latte or cappuccino is often twice the size of the Italian one.
  • The table below gives typical ranges. When your café's menu says the size, trust the menu.

Why the same drink changes between cafés and countries

  • Flat white. Australia and New Zealand both claim it, and both date it to the 1980s. Cup sizes reported for it are about 175 ml in New Zealand and about 200 ml in Australia. In Europe, a champion barista quoted by Perfect Daily Grind put his preferred size at 160 to 180 ml, noted that Belgian flat whites run a little larger than Hungarian ones, and said drinks in Asia can be larger again. US chains offer "large" flat whites, which departs from the original small size.
  • Cappuccino. Italy's espresso institute describes about 25 ml of espresso with about 100 ml of milk, which the steamer takes up to roughly 125 ml. Competition wording has used 150 to 180 ml. In the US, chain cappuccinos of 12 oz (360 ml) or more are common.
  • Latte. In Italy, asking for "a latte" gets you a glass of milk. Elsewhere it is the biggest and mildest of the espresso and milk drinks, often 8 to 12 oz and sometimes much larger.
  • Gibraltar and cortado. The gibraltar comes from San Francisco, where the drink took its name from the glass. A cortado is the broader name: in Spain the word means espresso "cut" with a little milk, and sizes and ratios vary by place. In Cuba and Miami, a cortadito is made with sweetened condensed milk.
  • Americano and long black. The long black is the Australian and New Zealand version: usually a double shot poured onto hot water. An americano adds hot water to the espresso, and the quantity of water is left to taste.

Because of all this, ordering by name in an unfamiliar café can get you a different size from the one you expected. If the menu lists millilitres or ounces, use those.

How to make each at home

Start with a normal double shot: for many home baskets that is about 18 g of coffee making about 36 g of espresso, see the ratio guide for ristretto, normale and lungo, the basket guide for the right dose for your basket, and the ratio calculator to work out the yield. If the shot itself tastes sour or bitter, sort that first in the dial-in helper: milk hides some faults and not others. Milk amounts below are cold milk and starting points. Steaming adds volume (Italy's spec takes 100 ml of cold milk up to about 125 ml), and a finishing temperature of about 55 to 65 °C is the range commonly quoted.

  • Espresso macchiato. One shot (or two if you like it longer). Add about 1 to 2 teaspoons of milk, lightly foamed, on top.
  • Cortado. A double shot with about 60 ml of cold milk. Steam gently with very little air so the milk is warm and smooth, with almost no foam.
  • Gibraltar. The same method as a cortado, poured into a 4.5 oz glass up to the rim. Without the glass, make a cortado.
  • Piccolo. One ristretto-style shot (a short, concentrated one, about 1:1 to 1:1.5) with about 60 to 80 ml of milk. Add only a brief burst of air, for thin microfoam, and pour into a small glass.
  • Flat white. A double shot with about 100 to 130 ml of cold milk. Add air briefly at the start, then swirl so the milk is glossy, with a layer of microfoam about 5 mm thick, and pour it so it mixes with the espresso.
  • Cappuccino. One or two shots with about 100 to 120 ml of cold milk. Add air for longer to build a thick, airy foam, aiming for a cap about 1 cm deep, in a cup of about 150 to 180 ml.
  • Latte. A double shot with about 180 to 250 ml of cold milk. Add air briefly, with a thin microfoam layer of about 5 mm on top, in a tall glass or large cup.
  • Americano. A double shot in a cup, then about 120 to 180 ml of hot water, adjusted to taste.
  • Long black. About 100 to 120 ml of hot water in the cup first, then pull a double shot straight onto it.

Too much or too little foam is mostly about how long the steam tip is near the surface at the start. Practise with the drink you like most, because small amounts of milk are harder to steam well than large ones.

Questions and answers

Is a flat white stronger than a latte?

Usually in flavour, not in caffeine. A flat white is typically made with a double shot in a smaller cup, around 5 to 7 oz (150 to 200 ml), while a latte is more like 8 to 12 oz (240 to 350 ml) or more, so the coffee taste is more pronounced in the flat white. With the same number of shots, the caffeine is similar. Sizes vary a lot by café, and many chains serve large flat whites, which narrows the gap.

What is the difference between a cortado and a macchiato?

A cortado is espresso cut with roughly an equal amount of steamed milk, giving a small, mellow drink of about 3.5 to 5 oz with little or no foam. An espresso macchiato is an espresso marked with only a spoonful or two of milk or foam, so it is mostly espresso and about 2 to 3 oz. The cortado is milky; the macchiato still tastes like espresso.

What is a gibraltar and is it the same as a cortado?

A gibraltar is a cup name as much as a recipe: it is the espresso and steamed milk drink served in a 4.5 oz (about 135 ml) Libbey Gibraltar glass, popularised by Blue Bottle Coffee in San Francisco. It is close to a cortado, with espresso and milk in roughly equal parts. Some people say the glass is the only difference and others say a gibraltar must be in that glass, and sources differ on the exact shots and milk.

What is the difference between a flat white and a cappuccino?

Both are fairly small, but the milk texture differs. A cappuccino has a thick, airy foam cap over the steamed milk (competition wording has asked for at least 1 cm), while a flat white has a thin, glossy layer of microfoam, about 5 mm, blended through the milk. A flat white is usually made with a double shot and a cappuccino with one or two.

Is an Italian cappuccino the same size as an American one?

No. Italy's national espresso institute describes about 25 ml of espresso with about 100 ml of milk steamed up to roughly 125 ml, in a cup of about 150 to 160 ml, and competition wording has used 150 to 180 ml. Many US chains serve 12 oz (about 360 ml) or larger, which means far more milk. If you want the small version, look for a café that serves it in a traditional cup.
