Why is my espresso sour?

Sour espresso is usually under-extracted: the water pulled too little out of the coffee. Grind finer, pull a longer ratio or use hotter water. Light roasts taste sour more easily when they are under-extracted, so they often need all three. Every number here is a starting point, and your beans, grinder and machine come first.

The usual fix, in order

  1. Measure the shot. Weigh the dose and the liquid in the cup, and time the shot from when you start the pump (the shot timer does this). A common starting point is a 1:2 ratio, such as 18 g in and 36 g out, in about 25 to 30 seconds. See the ratio cheat sheet.
  2. Ran faster than about 25 seconds? Grind one small step finer and keep the dose and yield the same.
  3. Time already in range? Make the grind step very small. If it is still sour, run the shot 2 to 4 g longer.
  4. Still sour? Look at the water temperature, then the beans, then puck preparation. The table below says how.

Change one thing at a time, and taste after each change. If the cup tastes good, the numbers do not matter.

Causes of sour espresso, most likely first

The order is a rule of thumb for home machines. Your own shot may differ, so use the time and taste to pick the row that fits.
CauseHow to recognize itFix
1. Grind too coarse (shot too fast) The shot runs fast, under about 25 seconds for a 1:2 shot, and under 20 seconds is a gusher. Thin body, a sharp taste and little sweetness. Check the time first: it tells you whether to look at this row or at rows 3 to 5. Grind one small step finer. Run a few grams through the grinder after the change, then taste. Repeat until the shot lands in the 25 to 30 second window. Do not overshoot: a grind that is too fine makes a shot that drips or chokes. A fast shot can also come from too little coffee in the basket (row 6) or an uneven puck. If the shot is already in range or slow and still sour, the grind is probably not the cause.
2. Ratio too short The time looks fine, but the shot was stopped early, for example 18 g in and 24 g out (1:1.3). Intense but sharp, with little sweetness. Let the shot run longer, toward 1:2 in 2 to 4 g steps. If you are already at 1:2, keep adding 2 to 4 g to the yield. Lighter roasts often taste better at 1:2.5 or more.
3. Water too cool Sour and thin even though the time and ratio are normal. Often worst on the first shot of the day or just after the machine switched on. Let the machine warm up fully, as its manual says. Flush water through the group and portafilter, and warm the cup. If your machine has a temperature setting, raise it 1 to 2 °C (about 2 to 4 °F) at a time. Guides often give about 90 to 96 °C (195 to 205 °F) for brewing, and one notes that entry-level machines can fall short of it.
4. Very fresh or light-roast beans Bright, tart, fruity sharpness from a light roast, or from beans only a few days off roast. Very fresh beans still release gas, which can make shots uneven. Give very fresh beans a few more days. For a light roast, try a longer ratio (1:2.5 or more), then a finer grind or hotter water. Some brightness is intended in a light roast. Sharp, thin and not sweet is the fault.
5. Channeling Sour and bitter in the same cup. Jets or spurts, pale streaks in the stream, or a cracked, pitted puck afterward. Water found a shortcut, so part of the coffee was barely touched. Fix puck preparation first: break up clumps, level the bed, tamp level, and do not knock the portafilter after tamping. A grind change will not fix a badly prepared puck. See "Channeling" in the troubleshooting chart.
6. Under-dosed basket Lots of empty space above the puck, a shot that gushes, and a thin, sour cup. Check the dose your basket is made for and weigh your dose. If there is a lot of space, add 0.5 g. See the basket size guide.

Sour or bitter: how to tell

The two are easy to mix up. Take a sip, then swallow, and notice where the unpleasant part is.

CompareSourBitter
FeelsSharp, puckering, tart, like an unripe fruitHarsh, drying, like strong black tea or burnt toast
WhenAt the start of the sip, and it fades fastIn the aftertaste, and it lingers
BodyThin, with little sweetnessCan be heavy, or thin and ashy
Usually meansUnder-extractedOver-extracted
First fixGrind finer, or run the shot longerGrind coarser, or stop the shot earlier

Both at once usually means channeling (row 5 above). If it tastes bitter instead, read why your espresso is bitter.

Questions and answers

Why is my espresso sour even though the shot time is right?

The clock is only a guide. A shot can run 25 to 30 seconds and still taste sour if it was stopped too early (a ratio well under 1:2), if the water is too cool, if the beans are a light roast that needs more extraction, or if the water found channels through the puck. Check the ratio first, then the water temperature, then puck preparation, and change one thing at a time.

Does grinding finer always fix sour espresso?

No. Finer is the first fix when the shot ran fast, under about 25 seconds. If the shot is already slow, over 30 seconds, going finer will not help and can make the machine choke. Look at channeling, water temperature and a longer ratio instead.

Why does light roast espresso taste sour?

Light roasts usually need more extraction to taste sweet, so they turn sharp and sour more easily when under-extracted. Guides suggest a longer ratio such as 1:2.5 or more, and sometimes a finer grind or hotter water. Some brightness is normal in a light roast, so aim for bright and sweet rather than sharp and thin.

Can very fresh beans make espresso sour?

They can. Beans only a few days off roast are still releasing gas, which can make shots uneven and thin. If that is your case, give them a few more days and try again. At the other end, old beans taste flat, so check the roast date on the bag.

How big a grind change should I make?

One small step on the grinder dial, keeping the dose and yield the same. Run a few grams of coffee through the grinder after the change, because grounds from the old setting are still inside, then taste again.
