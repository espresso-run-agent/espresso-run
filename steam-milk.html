How to steam milk at home

Microfoam for flat whites, lattes and cappuccinos with a home espresso machine's steam wand: the steps, how much air each drink needs, what to do when it goes wrong, and what to do if you have no steam wand. Every number is a starting point. Machines, wands and milks differ, so check your machine's manual.

The short answer

  • Start cold. Cold milk in a cold pitcher gives you more time before the milk gets too hot.
  • Two phases. First a short stretch: the tip just under the surface, adding air with a soft hiss. Then texturing: the tip a little deeper, the milk spinning in a whirlpool, so the bubbles shrink into glossy microfoam.
  • Stop at about 60 to 65 °C (140 to 150 °F). Hotter than that and the milk tastes scalded.
  • The drink decides the air. A flat white and a latte want little; a cappuccino wants more. See the table by drink.
  • Tap, swirl, pour soon. Foam separates from the milk if it sits.

Step by step with a steam wand

  1. Cold milk, cold pitcher. Use milk straight from the fridge in a stainless steel pitcher, and rinse the pitcher in cold water first if it has been sitting warm. Colder milk gives you longer to work before it overheats, and this matters most on machines with powerful steam. Shake plant milk cartons well, because they separate in the carton.
  2. Fill the pitcher a third to a half. The milk grows as it takes in air, so it needs room, and a whirlpool will not form in a pitcher that is too full. Guides give about one third as the usual starting point, and one Sage-machine guide says between a third and a half. The milk should still be deep enough to cover the steam tip. As a rough guide, a 350 ml (12 oz) pitcher suits one flat white or cappuccino, and a 600 ml (20 oz) pitcher suits a latte. The milk drinks guide gives the amount of cold milk for each drink.
  3. Purge the wand. Before the tip goes in the milk, open the steam valve briefly (a second or two) over the drip tray to blow out condensed water, so you start with dry steam. Close it again.
  4. Put the tip just under the surface, off to one side. About 1 cm below the surface is a common figure, slightly off-centre, with the pitcher tilted a little so the milk can circle. Open the steam fully if your machine's manual says to. Open it only once the tip is under the milk, as on some machines the first burst can splash.
  5. Stretch. Hold the tip right at the surface, so you hear a gentle hiss or tearing-paper sound (some call it chirping or "kissing"). As the milk grows, lower the pitcher a little to keep the tip at the surface. Stop after a few seconds, and sooner for a flat white or latte; see the table below for how long. Loud splattering or screeching means the tip is too high.
  6. Texture. Raise the pitcher so the tip sits deeper, around 2 cm under the surface, keeping the tilt so the milk keeps spinning. The hiss should go quiet and the milk should roll round in a smooth whirlpool. This folds the air in and shrinks the bubbles. Sources give about 8 to 12 seconds of this on one guide and 15 to 20 seconds on another, depending on the machine.
  7. Stop at 60 to 65 °C (140 to 150 °F). Use a thermometer while you learn. Without one, stop when the pitcher is too hot to hold comfortably. Close the steam valve while the tip is still in the milk, then lift the pitcher away.
  8. Wipe and purge the wand straight away. Wipe it with a clean damp cloth, then open the valve briefly to clear milk from inside the tip. Dried milk blocks the holes.
  9. Tap and swirl. Tap the pitcher firmly on the counter once or twice to pop large bubbles, then swirl it for several seconds until the surface looks glossy, like wet paint, with no visible bubbles.
  10. Pour soon. Foam and milk separate as they stand. A figure often quoted is to start pouring within about 10 seconds, and re-swirl if you need to wait a moment. If it has separated, swirl harder rather than re-steaming.

If your pitcher feels warm to the hand at the end of the stretch (about body temperature), that is the cue some guides use to switch from stretching to texturing. It is a better cue than counting seconds, because machines differ.

How long to stretch for each drink

The foam depths here are the ones in the milk drinks guide: about 5 mm of microfoam for a flat white, about 0.5 cm for a latte and a cap of about 1 cm for a cappuccino. What changes between them is how much air goes in during the stretch. Sources rarely give a time for each drink, so the seconds below are my starting points, based on one guide that stretches for about 2 to 3 seconds and one that stretches for about 5 seconds with cappuccino-style foam growing the milk by about 30%.

Two ways to judge it without a clock. By volume: stop stretching when the milk has grown by the amount you want (a bit for a flat white, about a third for a cappuccino). By feel: the pitcher is warm, not hot, at the end of the stretch. Whichever you use, change one thing at a time and write down what you did.

Troubleshooting: what went wrong with the milk

Big bubbles that will not go away Too much air in the stretch, or the tip stayed at the surface too long Shorten the stretch. Move the tip deeper and make a steady whirlpool. Tap the pitcher and swirl. A few seconds less air is the first change to try.

Which milk to use

  • Whole milk. The one most guides recommend for a first attempt: it gives creamy, stable microfoam and is the most forgiving.
  • Lower-fat and skim milk. They foam quickly and can give a lot of foam, but the foam is described as thinner and less smooth, and it blends into the milk less well.
  • Plant milks. Buy the "barista" edition where there is one. These have added stabilizers that help them steam, pour and stay mixed, and guides say non-barista versions do not steam as well. Each brand uses its own recipe, so the same type can behave differently from brand to brand. Oat is the one most often called easiest; soy foams well but can curdle with espresso if temperatures are not managed; almond tends to separate. Plant milks usually foam faster than dairy, so give them less air at the start, shake the carton well, and swirl the espresso before pouring. Try a lower stop temperature if one splits.

No steam wand? French press or handheld frother

You can make foam without a steam wand, but be honest about the result: it is coarser. Bubbles are bigger, the foam does not last as long, and you cannot get the glossy, paint-like microfoam for latte art. It suits a cappuccino or a hot milk drink better than a flat white.

  • French press (hot milk). Warm the milk in a pan or microwave to about 60 to 65 °C (140 to 150 °F), without boiling. Pour it into the press so that it is no more than a third full, with enough milk to cover the plunger's filter. Pump the plunger up and down briskly, keeping the filter under the surface, until the milk has roughly doubled. Tap, swirl and pour.
  • French press (cold milk first). America's Test Kitchen found that pumping cold milk gives smaller bubbles and a longer-lasting foam than pumping hot milk, because the milk proteins in hot milk have already bound together and coat the bubbles less well. Its method is to fill the glass beaker no more than a third with cold milk, pump for about 20 seconds until it doubles, then remove the plunger and heat the beaker in the microwave for 30 to 45 seconds. Only do this if your beaker is glass and the maker says it is microwave-safe.
  • Handheld whisk frother. Warm the milk to about 60 to 68 °C (guides vary), then hold the whisk head just under the surface, not touching the bottom or sides, in a tall jug with plenty of room. Foam for roughly 15 to 45 seconds, since guides vary, and stop when it has a layer of foam you like. If the head sits at the bottom it only stirs.
  • Pump frothers and sealed jars. One source says pump frothers make finer, longer-lasting foam than a handheld whisk, but still coarser than a steam wand; follow the maker's fill line. America's Test Kitchen describes shaking cold milk in a tightly sealed jar for about 20 seconds as giving a slightly coarser foam.

Hot milk in glass: let the beaker cool a little before you handle it.

Safety and cleaning

  • Steam, the steam wand and hot milk can all burn skin. Keep your hands clear of the tip, and hold the pitcher by its handle.
  • Keep the tip under the milk while you open and close the valve, so it does not spray.
  • Wipe the wand with a clean damp cloth straight after steaming, then purge it for a second or two. Milk dried on the wand blocks the holes and weakens the steam.
  • Rinse the pitcher straight away, and clean the wand and pitcher as your machine's manual says.

Questions and answers

How hot should steamed milk be?

Most guides stop at about 60 to 65 °C (140 to 150 °F), and a few go up to about 68 °C. Past that the foam gets less stable and the milk can taste scalded or burnt; one guide puts the burnt taste at about 70 °C. If you have no thermometer, stop when the pitcher is too hot to hold comfortably for more than a moment. Check your machine's manual too, because some home machines come with their own advice.

How long does it take to steam milk?

On a machine with strong steam, the air-adding stretch is a few seconds and the texturing whirlpool is roughly 8 to 20 seconds, so about 10 to 25 seconds in all for one drink. Machines with weak steam take longer, which is why they do best with small amounts of milk. Go by the temperature and the look of the milk rather than by the clock.

Why is my steamed milk full of big bubbles?

Usually too much air went in during the stretch, or the steam tip stayed too close to the surface for too long. Stretch for less time, move the tip deeper to start a steady whirlpool, then tap the pitcher on the counter and swirl it. If the bubbles are still there after that, the milk is better used for a cappuccino than for a flat white.

Can I make microfoam without a steam wand?

Not true microfoam. A French press, a handheld whisk frother or a pump frother gives a coarser foam with bigger bubbles that does not last as long, which suits a cappuccino or a hot milk drink better than a flat white or latte art. For the best result, warm the milk gently, keep the foaming tool just under the surface, leave room for the milk to grow, and pour straight away.

Which plant milk steams best?

Guides most often name oat milk as the easiest, followed by soy, and say to buy the barista edition, because these carry added stabilizers that help them steam and stay mixed. Plant milks tend to foam faster than dairy, so stretch for less time, and brands differ, so the same type can behave differently. Almond milk is usually described as the hardest because it tends to separate.
